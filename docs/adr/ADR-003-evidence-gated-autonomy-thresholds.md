# ADR 003: Evidence-Gated Autonomy — Complexity-Proportional Thresholds

## 1. Executive Brief
A learned (`low`-authority) policy never earns unsupervised execution from raw preference alone. It must pass a conjunctive set of statistical gates — scored-outcome count, a 95% Wilson lower-bound on correctness, mean reward, winner probability, probability margin, and snapshot stability — and those gates scale with a declared, persisted complexity profile rather than applying one global threshold. This is a real, mathematically fully-specified policy already implemented and documented in `docs/autonomy-complexity.md`/`docs/decision-making.md`, but it has never been captured as a decision record naming what was rejected — specifically, a single global autonomy threshold, and inferring complexity from the agent's own free-text reasoning rather than a declared profile.

## 3. Context
- Complexity is declared, not inferred: a persisted profile (`intent_ambiguity`, `context_variability`, `outcome_observability`, `decision_impact`, `reversibility`, `requires_human_approval`, `rationale`) is validated and stored per policy — `docs/autonomy-complexity.md`'s own stated reason: "The agent must not grade the complexity of its own answer from free text. That would make autonomy sensitive to wording and create an incentive to understate risk."
- The complexity score is a deterministic sum of ordinal dimensions (ambiguity/variability/observability 0–2 each; impact 0–3, reversibility 0–2, action-space size 0–2), mapped to four tiers (`low` 0–2, `standard` 3–6, `high` 7–9, `critical` 10–13), confirmed directly against the real table in `docs/autonomy-complexity.md`.
- Three **risk floors** override the additive score regardless of total: critical impact always forces `critical`; high-impact irreversible decisions are `critical`; high ambiguity plus subjective outcomes are at least `high`. These are hand-set exceptions to a formula that would otherwise let a severe single dimension be averaged away by otherwise-mild ones — confirmed directly, not inferred.
- The real, tier-scaled gate table (confirmed directly, `docs/autonomy-complexity.md`):

  | Criterion | Low | Standard | High | Critical |
  |---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
  | Scored outcomes | 3 | 20 | 50 | 100 |
  | 95% Wilson correctness lower bound | 40% | 90% | 95% | 97.5% |
  | Mean aggregate reward | > 0.00 | > 0.00 | > 0.10 | > 0.20 |
  | Winner probability | not gated | 60% | 75% | 85% |
  | Margin over runner-up | not gated | 15 pts | 30 pts | 45 pts |
  | Consecutive stable snapshots | 1 | 3 | 4 | 5 |
  | Drift-audit rate | 5% | 10% | 25% | 50% |

  The first six criteria (scored outcomes through stable snapshots) are conjunctive entry gates — all six must pass for that tier, not a weighted average. The seventh row, drift-audit rate, is a post-autonomy sampling parameter, not an entry gate — see below.
- `requires_human_approval: true` is a deterministic override, independent of every statistical gate above — `docs/autonomy-complexity.md`: "adds a failing autonomy criterion regardless of probability or outcome history. The SDK reports the block; it never silently converts approval into a statistical threshold."
- **Checked directly (`autonomy.py:472-476`), and `requires_human_approval: true` overrides *both* authorization paths, not only the statistical one:** `explicitly_approved = user_feedback_status == "accepted" and not complexity.profile.requires_human_approval`. A policy with `requires_human_approval: true` can never become eligible through explicit user acceptance either — accepting one episode does not "unlock" anything while that flag is set. §4.4 below is corrected accordingly.
- Explicit user acceptance is a *separate, non-statistical* authorization path, not a shortcut through the gates (when `requires_human_approval` is false) — a completed episode with `metadata.feedback_status: accepted` grants autonomy immediately, sets `authorization_basis: user_acceptance`, and sets the audit rate to zero; a later `rejected` episode revokes it and returns the policy to statistical gating. Confirmed directly against both `docs/autonomy-complexity.md` and `docs/decision-making.md`, and against `autonomy.py`'s own code.
- **The "drift-audit rate" row is not one of the conjunctive statistical gates.** Checked directly: `AutonomyAssessment.audit_rate` (`autonomy.py:218`) is a field separate from `criteria` (`autonomy.py:220`, the dict every pass/fail entry lives in), set by `audit_rate=0.0 if explicitly_approved else config.audit_rate` (`autonomy.py:499`) — it is never a pass/fail requirement a policy must clear to become eligible. It is the ongoing random-sampling rate applied *after* eligibility is granted. §4.3 is corrected to name this distinction explicitly.
- All numeric thresholds are overridable via environment variables (`AGENT_LEARNING_AUTONOMY_MIN_OUTCOMES` etc.), globally, not per-tier — confirmed in `docs/decision-making.md`'s own table. **Checked directly what "globally" actually means (`config.py:506-540`, `AutonomyConfig.for_tier()`): every tier's lookup reads the *same* environment-variable name**, so setting `AGENT_LEARNING_AUTONOMY_MIN_OUTCOMES=10` does not override a single default — it flattens `low`/`standard`/`high`/`critical` to 10 each, collapsing the entire complexity-proportional design for that one criterion. This is a real operational footgun, not a theoretical one, and is named as such in §6.
- **New finding, not previously documented anywhere: `assess_autonomy()` (`autonomy.py`) has no branch on `decision_authority` at all**, and is called identically for both `low`- and `full`-authority policies (`cli.py:543`, `596`, `653`, `860`, confirmed directly — none of these call sites checks authority before calling it). Because `full`-authority policies are explicitly excluded from REINFORCE training (`cli.py:334-343`: "full decision authority uses reasoned resolution, not REINFORCE"), their `PolicySnapshot.logits` never move from their initial values — so the `action_probability`/`probability_margin` criteria in §3's table, computed from those frozen logits, are only meaningfully exercised for `low`-authority policies — not driven by any learning process for `full` authority, where they are structurally close to permanently unmet rather than a real signal. This is addressed in a new §4.5 (using this same wording throughout, rather than the vaguer "inert," for precision).

## 4. Decision

### 4.1 Complexity is declared and validated, never inferred from the agent's own output
A policy's complexity profile is supplied at `task-policy-init`/`task-policy-complexity-set` time and persisted — it is never derived by asking the model to self-assess the riskiness of its own answer. This closes a real incentive problem: a self-grading agent would be incentivized to understate risk to unlock autonomy sooner. **Who may declare it is also part of this decision, not left implicit:** a complexity profile is governance-controlled metadata supplied by a policy owner, administrator, or trusted orchestration layer at policy-configuration time — never generated by, or on behalf of, the policy being evaluated. The CLI enforces this structurally rather than by convention: `task-policy-complexity-set` is a separate, explicit operator command, not a parameter the decision-making call itself (`task-policy-decide`) can set.

### 4.2 Risk floors override the additive score, not just inform it
The three named risk floors (§3) exist because a purely additive score could let one severe dimension (e.g., `critical` impact) be diluted by two mild ones (e.g., `low` ambiguity, `stable` context) into a merely "high" or "standard" total. The floors are a deliberate asymmetry: severity can only push the tier up, never be averaged down.

### 4.3 The six entry gates are conjunctive and scale monotonically by tier; drift-audit rate is a separate, post-autonomy sampling parameter
A policy cannot trade a stronger Wilson bound for a weaker outcome count, or vice versa — all six entry criteria (scored outcomes, Wilson correctness lower bound, mean reward, winner probability, margin, stable snapshots) must individually pass. The tiers scale monotonically (every one of those six gets strictly stricter from `low` through `critical`, never looser) rather than allowing, say, a `critical`-impact decision with a `low`-outcome-count requirement. **The seventh row in §3's table, drift-audit rate, is not part of this conjunction** (§3) — it is read only after eligibility is already decided, to set how often an autonomous decision still gets sampled for human review.

### 4.4 Deterministic approval is an absolute override; explicit acceptance is a conditional second path, not an independent one
`requires_human_approval: true` is a hard override that statistics cannot satisfy by definition — true to this extent as originally drafted. **Corrected here:** it is not merely "one gate among several" that explicit user acceptance can route around. Checked directly (§3), acceptance only grants autonomy when `requires_human_approval` is *also* false (`explicitly_approved = user_feedback_status == "accepted" and not requires_human_approval`) — so the two are not parallel, independently-satisfiable bases. `requires_human_approval` dominates: when true, neither the statistical path nor the explicit-acceptance path can grant autonomy, full stop. When false, explicit acceptance is a genuine, separate fast path that doesn't require clearing the six statistical gates. This is still a real, deliberate design (a human-required decision cannot be pre-approved by accepting one past instance of it), just not the "two independent bases" framing originally written.

### 4.5 The statistical gate table's probability-derived criteria are only meaningfully exercised for `low`-authority policies; real-time human adjudication is the actual mechanism for `full` authority
Checked directly, not previously documented anywhere in this chain: `assess_autonomy()` is invoked identically regardless of `decision_authority` — it never branches on it (`autonomy.py`), and every CLI call site invokes it unconditionally for both `low` and `full` policies (`cli.py:543/596/653/860`). But a `full`-authority policy's `PolicySnapshot.logits` are permanently excluded from REINFORCE (`cli.py:334-343`), so they never move from their initial values — meaning the `action_probability`/`probability_margin` gates in §3's table, computed from those frozen logits, are not driven by any learning process for a `full`-authority policy; they remain structurally close to permanently unmet, not a real signal about that policy's fitness for autonomy. This ADR does not treat that as a bug to fix here — it is a disclosed scope clarification: the *real*, working authorization mechanism for `full`-authority decisions is case-by-case human adjudication via `task-policy-adjudicate`, which the CLI itself enforces as a hard requirement (`cli.py:752`: this command only runs against `DecisionAuthority.FULL` policies). §3's gate table, in practice, governs `low`-authority autonomy; for `full` authority, `requires_human_approval` (§4.4) and per-instance adjudication are what actually gate execution, not this table.

**Potential future ADR, named but not scoped here:** whether `full`-authority policies warrant their own, distinct autonomy-governance mechanism — something other than "reasoning plus per-instance adjudication every time" — is a legitimate question this finding raises, not one it answers. Today's design may be entirely intentional (a reasoned decision arguably *should* get human eyes every time, by the same logic that makes `full` authority appropriate for it in the first place per ADR-001 §4.2). A future "ADR on autonomy governance for full-authority policies" is the right place to settle that, if it ever becomes worth settling — not an implicit assumption buried in this ADR.

## 5. Alternatives Considered

| Alternative | Reason for rejection |
|---|---|
| One global autonomy threshold for every decision | Named explicitly in `docs/autonomy-complexity.md`'s own opening framing as "too permissive for consequential decisions and too expensive for routine ones" — the real, stated reason the complexity-tier system exists at all. |
| Infer complexity from the agent's own reasoning/explanation text | Creates a direct incentive to understate risk to reach autonomy sooner, and makes autonomy sensitive to wording rather than to declared, auditable facts about the decision. |
| A weighted/averaged risk score instead of hard risk floors | Would let a single critical-impact dimension be diluted by unrelated mild dimensions — the three named floors exist specifically to prevent this. |
| Per-tier environment-variable overrides instead of global ones | Not implemented this way; the simpler global-override design was chosen — operational simplicity and configuration consistency over per-tier tuning flexibility. **Named honestly per §3/§6: this is a real, confirmed footgun** (one env var flattens all four tiers for that criterion), not a cost-free simplification — the per-policy complexity profile remains the only genuinely tier-specific lever, and it doesn't reach the numeric thresholds themselves. |
| Automatic complexity reclassification from historical outcome data (e.g., a policy that performs well gets "promoted" to a lower tier automatically) | Rejected: historical success rate measures how well a policy has performed, not the intrinsic consequence or reversibility of the decision it's making — those are what complexity is meant to capture (§3/§4.1). Collapsing performance into risk classification would re-introduce exactly the self-grading incentive problem §4.1 exists to prevent, just mediated through outcomes instead of free text. |

## 6. Consequences

### Positive
- The conjunctive, tier-scaled gate design means a `critical`-tier decision cannot drift into autonomy on a technicality (one strong criterion compensating for a weak one) — every real threshold must independently hold.
- Declared-not-inferred complexity removes a real self-assessment incentive problem before it could occur, rather than detecting and correcting it after the fact.
- `requires_human_approval` dominating both authorization paths (§4.4) means a decision correctly flagged as needing a human cannot be bypassed by accepting one past instance of it — the override is genuinely absolute, not softened by an adjacent fast path.

### Negative
- A legitimately low-risk decision that was *mis-profiled* at a higher complexity tier (human error at `task-policy-init` time) pays the full cost of that tier's stricter gates with no automatic correction — the system trusts the declared profile completely, by design (§4.1), which means a wrong declaration is not self-correcting.
- **Global (not per-tier) environment-variable overrides are a confirmed, real footgun, not just a missing convenience** (§3): setting `AGENT_LEARNING_AUTONOMY_MIN_OUTCOMES=10` flattens the outcome-count requirement for `low`/`standard`/`high`/`critical` alike, silently collapsing the complexity-proportional design for whichever criterion is overridden. An operator intending to loosen only `low`-tier routine decisions would, without realizing it, also loosen `critical`-tier ones.
- `full`-authority policies get the same `assess_autonomy()` statistical assessment attached to their decision output as `low`-authority ones, even though its probability-derived criteria are not driven by any learning process for them (§4.5) — a reader of raw CLI output who doesn't know this could mistake a `full`-authority policy's permanently-unmet statistical criteria for a real signal, when the actual authorization mechanism (adjudication) is entirely separate.

## 7. Architectural Invariants

1. Complexity profiles are externally declared and persisted, never inferred from the policy's own output (§4.1).
2. Complexity tier assignment is deterministic — the same profile always yields the same tier.
3. Risk floors may only elevate a tier, never lower one (§4.2).
4. All six statistical entry gates are conjunctive and scale monotonically by tier; drift-audit rate is not one of them (§4.3).
5. No higher-complexity tier may require weaker entry criteria than a lower-complexity tier, for any of the six gates — a future threshold change that violated this would be a regression, not a tuning choice (§4.3).
6. `requires_human_approval: true` blocks every authorization path, statistical and explicit-acceptance alike — it is absolute, not one gate among several (§4.4).
7. The statistical gate table's `action_probability`/`probability_margin` criteria are only meaningfully exercised for `low`-authority policies; `full`-authority execution is actually gated by per-instance adjudication (§4.5).
